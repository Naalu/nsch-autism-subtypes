## Subtypes from co-occurring conditions

Attempts to split autism by co-occurring conditions go back at least to Doshi-Velez, Ge and Kohane (2014), who clustered diagnosis-code trajectories of about 4,900 patients at one hospital and found three small distinct groups (seizure, multisystem, psychiatric) and one large unresolved group, with a replication sample from a second region. Lingren et al. (2016) reported psychiatric, developmental and seizure groupings in 20,658 hospital records. Vargason, Frye, McGuinness and Hahn (2019) applied k-means to insurance claims for 3,278 children and found three groups: high rates of everything (24%), developmental delay (27%), and low rates (50%). Montgomery et al. (2023) fit a latent profile model to 37 indicators in 754 children from the Australian Autism Biobank and found four profiles ordered by support need. Most recently Litman et al. (2025) fit a generative mixture model to more than 5,000 SPARK participants and reported four classes (social and behavioral challenges 37%, mixed autism with developmental delay 19%, moderate challenges 34%, broadly affected 10%) that replicated in an independent cohort and differed in genetic signal. Across these, the recurring contrasts are a developmental or language group, a psychiatric or behavioral group, and a small high-burden group; the number of classes varies with the inputs and the method.

## Why replication is the question

Agelink van Rentergem, Deserno and Geurts (2021) reviewed 156 autism subtyping studies from 2001 to 2020 and proposed a seven-item checklist of validation strategies. External validation on variables not used in clustering was common (88% of studies); replication in an independent sample (9%), a second method (13%), and parallel measures (6%) were rare, and no study used all seven. The concern is not academic. Bauer and Curran (2004) showed that latent class and growth mixture models recover spurious classes from data generated by a single continuous, non-normal distribution, and that fit indices favor more classes as sample size grows; Lubke and Muthén (2005) framed the comparison of class and factor models as the appropriate test. A subtype claim therefore needs two things a single clustering does not give: evidence that the groups return in other children, and evidence that a continuous description does not fit as well.

## Where this study sits

This study uses a nationally representative survey rather than a clinic or registry, so its class shares are population estimates rather than sample descriptions; it fixes its checks before fitting and adds a held-out survey year released after the design was frozen; and it fits the continuous alternative on the same likelihood scale. Its inputs are weaker than the clinic studies' (parent report of a provider's diagnosis, 18 yes/no items, one time point), which limits what it can say about any individual child and rules out temporal and predictive validation. What it can say is whether the structure others report is visible in the general population and whether that structure is better described as types or as dimensions.
