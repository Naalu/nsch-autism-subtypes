# Validation checklist

Clustering always produces groups, so a subtyping study has to show that its groups are more than an artifact of one sample and one algorithm. Agelink van Rentergem, Deserno and Geurts (2021) reviewed 156 autism subtyping studies published from 2001 to June 2020 and distilled the ways of showing that into a seven-item checklist, SUVAC (SUbtyping VAlidation Checklist). The table below says how this project handles each item and what happened. The right-hand column is the share of the 156 reviewed studies that used each strategy; no study used all seven.

The methods named in the table (ARI, bootstrap Jaccard, entropy) are explained in plain language in `docs/background.md`. Numbers below come from the StepMix (reference backend) runs of October 19 and 20, 2026, at K = 4, the number of classes chosen by the rule described under "Decisions" below. They will be re-verified with the from-scratch code. Intervals are 95% intervals from resampling children; they do not include the uncertainty from refitting the models, which the bootstrap row covers.

Two checks beyond SUVAC were added after the first results and use the same pass lines. Refits within age bands 6 to 11 and 12 to 17 agree with the pooled model (ARI 0.70 and 0.78; `scripts/09_age_bands.py`), and the two axes have the same shape in both bands (loading congruence 0.98 or higher; `scripts/12_dimensions.py`), so the classes are not an age artifact. The psychiatric score does rise with age (correlation 0.26), which matches when anxiety and depression are usually diagnosed. Robustness checks (`scripts/11_robustness.py`): a survey-weighted fit agrees with the unweighted one at ARI 0.78, and at 0.998 among the 62% of children both place confidently; the 819 children dropped for a blank answer are similar in age and sex to those kept and slightly more often rated severe (12% against 10%); refits from five other seeds agree at 0.996 or above.

## Pass lines and where they come from

- **ARI of at least 0.65.** Steinley (2004) gives rough bands for the adjusted Rand index: 0.90 and up excellent, 0.80 to 0.90 good, 0.65 to 0.80 moderate, below 0.65 poor. We use the floor of "moderate" because the comparisons are between different children (held-out years) or very different methods, where near-perfect agreement is not expected.
- **Mean bootstrap Jaccard of at least 0.75** for a stable class, 0.5 or below for a dissolved one, from Hennig (2007). 200 resamples at K = 4, 5, 6 and 8, twice the default in `fpc::clusterboot`; 100 at K = 7. For scale, random labels with our K = 4 class sizes score 0.14 to 0.23, so a smaller K gains little from chance alone; the more important caveat is in decision 1.
- **Number of classes.** The proposal named BIC (Nylund, Asparouhov and Muthén 2007). BIC did not settle, and the rule that replaced it is decision 1 below.

## Decisions taken after the first results

These are changes from the proposal, made after seeing results. The paper says so.

**1. Choice of K.** BIC fell at every K from 1 to 8 and would have chosen whatever upper limit we set. The drops shrink from 8,224 (K = 1 to 2) to 6 (K = 7 to 8), the column-shuffled baseline rises throughout, and classes added past K = 5 hold under 3% of children each. Held-out likelihood, which cannot reward overfitting, also keeps improving through K = 8 (`scripts/13_heldout_selection.py`), so neither criterion selects a K. Both say only that there is more structure than four classes capture, which decision 3 takes up.

We therefore choose K by stability: the largest K at which every class has mean bootstrap Jaccard of at least 0.75. The idea that a real cluster should reappear when the sample is redrawn is from Ben-Hur, Elisseeff and Guyon (2002) and Lange et al. (2004), and the 0.75 line is Hennig's. The least stable class at each K tested: 0.91 at K = 4, 0.62 at K = 5, 0.55 at K = 6, 0.66 at K = 7, 0.55 at K = 8. The rule gives K = 4 (`results/model_choice.json`).

What the rule can and cannot say was measured by simulation (`scripts/14_shape_and_stability.py`, 50 resamples and 10 starts per fit; `reports/figures/results/stability_vs_simulation.png`). On data simulated from the two-dimensional continuous model, which has no classes at all, every class is stable through K = 5 (least stable 0.86 at K = 4, 0.88 at K = 5) and stability breaks only at K = 6 (0.54). So stability does not separate groups from a continuum: a smooth cloud of children can be cut into four or five pieces that come back every time. K = 4 from this rule means "the finest division of these children that comes back reliably", and whether the pieces are groups is decided by the evidence in decision 3. Two things the simulation does show. On data simulated from the five-class model, all five classes are stable, including an 8% class (0.87), so the real fifth class failing (0.62) is not explained by its size. And the real data break at K = 5, where the two-dimensional continuum does not, which fits a weaker third direction (decision 3) that gives a fifth class two nearly equal ways to form.

An earlier draft of this rule also required the held-out-years replication to pass. We dropped that condition so the replication stays a test of the chosen classes rather than an input to choosing them; both versions give K = 4. The 2025 forward test was visible when the rule was set, so it should be read as confirmatory; it is not fully independent.

K = 5 is reported as a sensitivity analysis. It adds a small class (9% weighted) with intellectual disability, speech and developmental delay near 1, and the highest rates of epilepsy, cerebral palsy, Down syndrome, deafness and blindness; 42% of its children are rated severe. The class exists in every resample but its membership does not hold (Jaccard 0.62), the held-out-years replication fails (ARI 0.646, interval 0.63 to 0.67), and the survey-weighted fit disagrees (ARI 0.58). The other K = 5 checks pass (2025 0.75, age bands 0.77 and 0.83). Past K = 5 the replication and 2025 tests fail too (`reports/tables/checks_by_k.csv`).

**2. Cross-check method.** The proposal named Jaccard distance with average linkage. On this data it puts 94% of children in one cluster at each K from 4 to 6 (ARI 0.00 against the classes). Average linkage chains: with only 18 yes/no items, thousands of children sit at small distances from one another, so one cluster keeps absorbing its neighbors, and the 517 children with no conditions form a separate point. Complete linkage, prevalence-weighted Jaccard and spectral clustering on a Jaccard nearest-neighbor graph do no better (ARI 0.08 or less, with one cluster holding 58% to 90% of children), and Ward linkage reaches 0.24 (`reports/tables/method_comparison.csv`). Trees are cut with SciPy's `cut_tree`; `fcluster(..., "maxclust")` returns fewer clusters than asked when merges tie, which happens often with 0/1 data.

k-means on the flags is the reported second method, at 0.54 (below the line). k-means on these flags has several near-tied solutions: with 200 starts from each of five seeds, the best solutions differ in total distance by about 0.01% and agree with the classes at 0.54 to 0.57; with 20 starts, as first run, the range was 0.47 to 0.65. The disagreement is concentrated in children near a class boundary. Among children the class model places with probability 0.9 or more, k-means agrees at 0.93. Two methods that agree on the confident children and split the uncertain ones differently, with no clean gap for either to find, is what a mostly continuous structure predicts. The hierarchical implementation stays in the project because it is in the proposal, and reproducing SciPy's degenerate result will be how we know our version is correct.

**3. How much is grouped and how much is continuous.** Every method that found structure found the same two axes, so we fit a latent trait model (binary factor analysis; `src/nsch_subtypes/latent_trait.py`, `scripts/12_dimensions.py`) to the same data. Five results:

- Fit. The two-dimensional model has a lower BIC (105,291, 53 parameters) than any latent class model (106,428 for K = 4 with 75 parameters; 105,656 for K = 8 with 151). Three and four dimensions are lower still.
- New children. On held-out years, two dimensions predict as well as eight classes: fit on 2016 to 2021 and scored on 2022 to 2024, D = 2 trails the best model by 115 log-likelihood units (standard error 19) and K = 8 by 124 (22); scored on 2025, D = 2 trails by 40 (11) and K = 8 by 36 (12). K = 4 trails by 345 and 120.
- Axes. The two-dimensional slopes split the conditions into a developmental-language axis (developmental delay, intellectual disability, learning, speech) and a psychiatric axis (anxiety, depression, ADHD, behavior). A two-cut rule on the scores reproduces the K = 4 classes at ARI 0.79, with cut points set from the class means.
- A third direction. The three-dimensional model adds a medical-sensory axis (cerebral palsy, deafness, blindness, Down syndrome, epilepsy, headache). It is the same signal as the small K = 5 class, which is the class that does not hold together. Pairs of medical-sensory conditions (deafness with blindness, epilepsy with cerebral palsy) are among the pairs the K = 4 and D = 2 models fit worst, with ADHD and behavior problems, and the D = 3 model brings the pairs above 3.84 from 49 to 36 of 153.
- Simulation. Data simulated from the fitted two-dimensional model, with no classes in it, give a latent class BIC curve that keeps falling through K = 6, as the real one does. Data simulated from the K = 4 class model give a curve that turns up at K = 4, so the comparison can tell the two apart. The real data behave like the continuum (Bauer and Curran 2004).
- Shape of the cloud. The two-dimensional model assumes children's positions follow a bell curve. We also estimated the distribution of positions from the data (Laird 1978) and measured how much better it explains the children than the bell curve does. For five datasets simulated from the continuous model the gain is 16 to 23 log-likelihood units; for five simulated from the four-class model it is 177 to 242; for the real data it is 94 (`reports/figures/results/latent_density.png`). By that measure the real children are lumpier than a smooth bell curve and half as lumpy as four separate groups. In the picture, the real estimate gathers into about five spots laid out along the two axes, the class simulations into four tight spots, and the continuum simulations into a spread-out ring; the estimator is spiky by nature, so the number carries more weight than the picture. Some concentration of children at typical combinations, on top of continuous variation, is what a factor mixture model would describe, which is why that model is the natural next step.

The resulting description: four broad profiles, the combinations of high and low on the two main axes, replicate across years, in 2025, within age bands and across resampling. Their borders are soft. A continuous description fits better and predicts new children as well as twice as many classes, finer class solutions do not replicate, and the medical-sensory structure shows up as a third axis or as a small class whose membership is unstable. The four classes are a useful summary of a mostly continuous structure, not four distinct subtypes. `reports/figures/results/map.png` shows this directly: the children form one cloud, and the classes are regions of it, with the children whose class is uncertain lying along the borders. A factor mixture model (Lubke and Muthén 2005), which allows classes and continuous variation within them at the same time, is the natural next model and is left for future work.

**4. Reading the severity test.** The first commit states the planned test as "if `class` and `both` do not beat `count`, the classes are a gradient in disguise." That sentence can be read two ways, and we report both. Count plus class beats count alone on BIC (14,450 against 14,873) and on cross-validated log-loss (0.815 against 0.840). Class alone does not beat count (BIC 14,999). The comparator models added after the first results put this in context: the two continuous scores alone (BIC 14,262, 6 parameters) beat count plus class, and all 18 flags entered separately do best (14,159). The classes carry information about severity beyond the count, but less than the continuous scores do. Full table: `results_k4/count_vs_class.csv`.

## Related results to compare against

Unsupervised subtyping has no benchmark with ground truth, so "state of the art" means the published subtype solutions closest to ours and the strongest sensible methods on our own data.

| Lingren et al. 2016 | 20,658 autistic patients, records from three academic medical centers | Comorbidity codes | Clustering (secondary analysis) | Psychiatric, developmental, seizure | None reported |

Litman's four SPARK classes (37 / 19 / 34 / 10) and ours (18 / 33 / 34 / 16) are not a one-to-one match, but a nationally representative survey with 18 yes/no items landing on a similar broad shape is the closest comparison available. The "broadly affected" class that Vargason (24%) and Litman (10%) report corresponds to our K = 5 class, and our bootstrap says its membership is not stable in parent-reported data.

Two newer lines of work bear on the types-or-dimensions question. For core autism traits, Kim et al. (2019) found a dimensional structure fit best in 3,825 clinic-referred youth, while Wittkopf et al. (2023) found BIC preferred dimensions for ADOS data but the factor scores were multimodal, and argued for a hybrid. We found no study making the comparison for co-occurring conditions. And Zhang et al. (2025) found that autism's common-variant genetics splits into two factors, one tied to earlier diagnosis and early social and communication difficulty, the other to later diagnosis, adolescent socioemotional and behavioral difficulty, and ADHD and mental health conditions. That is a close counterpart to our two axes, including the age pattern: our psychiatric score rises with age (correlation 0.26) and the developmental score falls slightly (-0.11).

On our own data the method comparisons are: the from-scratch code against StepMix and SciPy (correctness), k-means and six other methods against the latent classes (second method), the column-shuffled baseline (no structure), the condition count against the classes on severity (the planned null), and the latent trait model against every K, in-sample and on held-out years (types or dimensions), and simulated data with no classes and with known classes as a yardstick for the stability and shape checks.
